# AI Governance Interoperability Index

## Mapping Fragmentation and Pathways for Inclusive Global AI Governance

## Project Overview

Artificial intelligence is now being governed through many different national, regional, and international approaches. Some countries and regions are creating binding laws, while others are using voluntary principles, policy guidance, standards, or sector-specific regulation. This has created a fast-moving global AI governance landscape that is active and innovative, but also fragmented.

This project examines selected AI governance frameworks and initiatives to understand how they approach responsible AI, risk management, institutional coordination, international cooperation, and inclusion. The purpose is not to rank one governance model as better than another. Instead, the project aims to compare how different approaches are structured, where they align, where they differ, and how they may support or limit interoperability.

In this study, interoperability means the ability of different AI governance systems to work together, even when they are not identical. This may include shared principles, comparable risk approaches, common standards, policy learning, institutional cooperation, and support for countries with different levels of digital capacity.

## Why This Study Was Conducted

AI systems, markets, risks, and supply chains increasingly operate across borders. However, AI governance is developing through different legal, institutional, and policy models. This creates a need to understand how these models can coexist, communicate, and cooperate without forcing every country or region into one single regulatory approach.

This study was conducted to support clearer understanding of the global AI governance “patchwork” and to explore practical pathways toward more inclusive and interoperable governance. It is designed as a policy synthesis and visual communication project for non-specialist and policy audiences.

## What This Project Aims to Achieve

The project aims to:

1. Map selected global, regional, and national AI governance frameworks.
2. Compare their governance philosophy, binding status, risk approach, institutional model, standards linkages, cooperation mechanisms, and inclusion focus.
3. Develop an interpretive AI Governance Interoperability Readiness Score.
4. Identify areas of alignment, fragmentation, and potential cooperation.
5. Present complex AI governance information in a clear, structured, and visually accessible format.

## Methodology

The project uses a structured comparative mapping approach. Each selected framework is reviewed across key governance dimensions, including:

* Governance level and region
* Instrument type
* Binding status
* Core governance philosophy
* Primary risk approach
* Institutional model
* Standards and assurance tools
* International cooperation mechanisms
* Inclusion and capacity-building focus
* Interoperability strengths and gaps

The project also applies an interpretive scoring model to assess how far each framework may support AI governance interoperability.

## Scoring Dimensions

The AI Governance Interoperability Readiness Score is based on six dimensions:

| Dimension                        | What It Measures                                                                                                                              |
| -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Shared Principles Alignment      | Alignment with responsible AI principles such as safety, transparency, accountability, fairness, privacy, inclusion, and human rights         |
| Risk Classification Clarity      | Clarity of risk categories, risk assessment methods, or risk management guidance                                                              |
| Institutional Coordination       | Presence of clear institutions, regulators, coordination bodies, or implementation mechanisms                                                 |
| Standards and Assurance Linkages | Connection to standards, testing, audits, impact assessments, certification, or risk management tools                                         |
| International Cooperation        | Support for cross-border cooperation, policy exchange, standards coordination, or multilateral dialogue                                       |
| Inclusion and Capacity Building  | Attention to developing countries, digital divides, public interest, marginalized groups, skills, infrastructure, and equitable participation |

## Scoring Scale

Each dimension is scored from 1 to 5.

| Score | Meaning                 | Interpretation                                                                   |
| ----: | ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
|     1 | Very limited or unclear | The dimension is mostly absent or not clearly explained                          |
|     2 | Emerging or partial     | The dimension is mentioned but not developed in detail                           |
|     3 | Moderate                | The dimension is present, but implementation or clarity is limited               |
|     4 | Strong                  | The dimension is clearly addressed and reasonably developed                      |
|     5 | Very strong             | The dimension is central, well-developed, and strongly supports interoperability |

The total score is calculated out of 30.

**Interoperability Readiness Percentage = Total Score / 30 × 100**

The scores are not official rankings. They are interpretive, evidence-based policy synthesis indicators designed to support structured comparison, discussion, and visual analysis.

## Key Analytical Questions

This project is guided by the following questions:

1. How are major AI governance approaches structured across global, regional, and national levels?
2. Which frameworks rely on binding regulation, voluntary guidance, standards, or policy coordination?
3. How clearly do different approaches define and manage AI risks?
4. Where do frameworks align around common principles?
5. Where does fragmentation appear in terminology, institutions, implementation, and capacity?
6. What practical pathways could support more inclusive AI governance interoperability?

## Limitations

This project is a compact portfolio study and does not cover every national AI policy or regulatory initiative. The scoring model is interpretive and based on selected public sources. It should be read as a structured synthesis tool, not as an official assessment or legal evaluation.

The analysis focuses on interoperability potential rather than overall policy quality. A high score does not mean a framework is perfect, and a lower score does not mean a framework is unimportant. Each framework reflects its own political, legal, institutional, and development context.
